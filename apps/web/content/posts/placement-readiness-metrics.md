Ask a placement cell how ready its batch is and the answer is usually a CGPA distribution. CGPA is easy to collect and many companies use it as an eligibility cut-off, so it matters. But it answers a narrow question: did the student do well in university examinations? It says little about whether they can solve a problem under time pressure, explain a project to a stranger, or write a resume a recruiter will read past the first line.

This guide is for Training and Placement Officers and heads of department who want a small, honest set of signals for placement readiness, collected once for the whole batch and tracked by department over a semester.

## Why a small set beats a dashboard

The temptation is to measure everything a platform can export: hours logged, problems attempted, videos watched. Resist it. Activity counts reward students who are good at looking busy, and a report with thirty columns gets read once.

Pick signals that meet three tests:

1. **Recruiters actually screen on it.** If no company's process looks at it, it is not a readiness signal.
2. **A student can improve it in a semester.** Otherwise tracking it only produces anxiety.
3. **It can be measured the same way for every student.** Different tests for different sections cannot be compared.

## Five signals worth tracking

### 1. Problem-solving practice

Most campus processes include an aptitude round and at least one coding round. Measure both with a short, timed, identical set: an aptitude section and one or two easy coding problems with hidden test cases. Record the score, not the number of practice problems attempted. A student who has attempted four hundred problems and cannot finish an easy one in thirty minutes is not ready, whatever the counter says.

### 2. One finished project

Not a list of repositories. One project the student built, that runs, and that they can explain: what it does, why they chose the approach, and what went wrong. Record three things: does it exist, is it deployed or runnable from its README, and can the student name one decision they made. The third is the one interviewers probe. [Real projects in coursework](/blogs/project-based-learning-cs) covers how faculty can make sure every student leaves a course with one.

### 3. Mock interview score

A structured mock, same questions for everyone, scored against a short rubric: clarity of explanation, handling of a follow-up question, and the project walkthrough. The score matters less than the trend between the first and second mock. [Mock interviews for a whole batch](/blogs/mock-interviews-at-scale) describes a rubric that works at scale.

### 4. Resume quality

Check every resume against a written standard rather than a reviewer's taste. A workable checklist: one page, readable when parsed as plain text, a skills section limited to what the student can defend, and project lines that describe outcomes and decisions. Score it pass, needs work or missing.

### 5. Communication

This overlaps with the mock score, and that is fine. Communication shows up in every round, from the group discussion some companies still run to the final HR conversation. The simplest honest measure is the "explain your project in two minutes" item from the mock rubric, scored by someone who did not build the project.

## Baselining a batch

Run all five in the first month of the semester, in one sitting of about an hour plus a short mock slot. Then summarise by department and section, not by student, for anyone outside the cell:

| Signal | Ready | Close | Not started |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem solving | cleared both | cleared one | cleared neither |
| Project | runnable, explained | exists, not explained | none |
| Mock | rubric average at target | below target | not taken |
| Resume | passes checklist | needs work | missing |

Three buckets are enough. Finer scales invite arguments about a single point that change nothing about what you do next.

Repeat the same measurement in the middle of the semester and again before the season opens. The movement between runs, per department, is the number heads of department should see. It shows whether an intervention worked, and it compares a department with itself instead of against another department with a different intake.

## Tracking without surveillance

Readiness tracking goes wrong when students experience it as monitoring. A few rules keep it useful:

- **Tell students what is measured and why**, before the baseline. No hidden signals.
- **Report aggregates outside the cell.** Faculty and heads of department need department and section numbers, not a ranked list of names. Individual results go to the student and to the coordinator who is helping them.
- **Do not track activity.** Login times, time spent on a page and keystrokes measure compliance, not readiness.
- **Let students see their own record** and correct it, such as a project that was not picked up.
- **Collect the minimum personal data you need**, and know where it is stored. India's data protection framework is set out by the [Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology](https://www.meity.gov.in/); read it before you put student records into a new tool.

A student who trusts the measurement uses it to decide what to work on. A student who does not will game it.

## Where CGPA still fits

Keep CGPA in the record, because many companies set an eligibility cut-off on it and students need to know which drives they qualify for. Just do not use it as the readiness measure. It is an input to eligibility; the five signals above are what decide whether an eligible student clears the rounds.

For outside reference points, the [National Association of Colleges and Employers](https://www.naceweb.org/) publishes research on the competencies employers look for in graduates, and [AICTE](https://www.aicte-india.org/) publishes the framework for internships and industry readiness in technical education. Both are worth a read before you settle on your own list.

## Using the numbers

The baseline is only worth running if it changes what the cell does. A department where most students have no project needs a coursework change, not more aptitude drills. A section that clears problem solving but scores low on the mock needs speaking practice. A batch with strong signals across the board needs better outreach, which is where [the semester plan for a placement season](/blogs/placement-season-plan) picks up.
