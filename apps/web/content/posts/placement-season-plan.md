Most placement seasons are run backwards. The first company confirms a date, the cell scrambles to collect resumes, a notice goes out on the student group, and the batch discovers in the same week that it needs an aptitude score, a project it can explain and a resume that fits on one page. By then the calendar has already decided the outcome.

This guide is a semester plan for Training and Placement Officers, heads of department and placement cell coordinators who want to run the season forward instead: know where the batch stands, fix what can be fixed while there is still time, and treat the drives themselves as logistics rather than a crisis.

The months below assume a season that opens around the start of the final year. Shift them to match your own calendar; the order matters more than the dates.

## Month one: take a readiness baseline

You cannot plan for a batch you have not measured. Before any outreach, run one short, identical baseline for every student who intends to sit for placements:

- a timed aptitude set of the kind most screening rounds use
- one or two easy coding problems, scored automatically
- a resume upload, checked against a simple written standard
- a short form: which project they would talk about in an interview, and a link to it

Keep it under an hour and make it the same for every department. The point is not to rank students. It is to find out, per department and per section, how many students are nearly ready, how many need a push, and how many have not started. [Measuring placement readiness](/blogs/placement-readiness-metrics) covers which signals to collect and how to read them without turning the exercise into surveillance.

Share the baseline with heads of department in the same week. A department that sees its own numbers is far more likely to free up lab hours than one that hears a general appeal.

## Month two: resume clinics and project follow-through

Resumes are the cheapest thing to fix and the first thing a recruiter sees, so fix them early. Run clinics by department, in groups of twenty or thirty, with a single written standard: one page, a skills section that matches what the student can defend in an interview, and projects described by what was built and what decision the student made, not by the technologies used.

The same month, chase projects. The baseline form tells you which students have nothing they can talk about. Those students need a small, finished project more than they need another course. [Real projects in coursework](/blogs/project-based-learning-cs) describes how faculty can assign work that doubles as interview material, which is the most scalable fix if the gap is batch-wide.

## Month three: mock interviews, first pass

Mock interviews do the most good before the season, not during it. A student who hears their own answer to "tell me about your project" for the first time in a real interview has already lost the round.

Run a first pass for the whole batch: a structured mock with the same questions for everyone, scored against a short rubric. At batch scale this is where AI voice mocks earn their place, because they let every student practise without a faculty member in the room. Keep faculty time for the final rounds. [Mock interviews for a whole batch](/blogs/mock-interviews-at-scale) has the full structure, including the rubric and how to route students from the first pass to faculty.

## Month four: company outreach with evidence

Outreach works better when you can tell a company something specific about the batch. "Eighty students from CSE and IT" is a list. "Sixty students who have cleared an aptitude and coding baseline and each have a deployed project" is a pitch.

Build a one-page profile per department from the baseline and the mock results. Keep it honest; recruiters talk to each other, and a cell that overstates its batch once is remembered for it. Send it with the dates you can offer, the rooms and lab capacity you have, and the name of the person who will actually answer the phone on drive day.

Two sources worth reading while you plan outreach: the [National Association of Colleges and Employers](https://www.naceweb.org/) publishes research on what employers look for in new graduates, and [AICTE](https://www.aicte-india.org/) maintains the approval and internship framework that many recruiters will ask about. If your institution participates in the [National Institutional Ranking Framework](https://www.nirfindia.org/), remember that graduation outcomes are part of what it publishes, so the numbers you collect this season have a second use.

## Months five and six: drive logistics

By now the batch is as ready as it will be, and the job becomes operations. The drives that go badly usually go badly for boring reasons:

- **Eligibility confusion.** Publish each company's criteria in writing, with the exact list of eligible students, at least three days before the drive. Disputes on the morning of a drive cost everyone the day.
- **Clashing drives.** Keep one shared calendar. Two companies on the same day split the strongest students and annoy both recruiters.
- **Lab and network failures.** Test every machine and the connection the day before an online test, with the actual test link if the company allows it.
- **Silent students.** Confirm attendance the evening before. A recruiter who flies in for forty students and meets twenty-two will not come back.

Brief students before every drive: the rounds, the timings, what to bring, and what the company does. [What companies look for in campus hiring](/blogs/campus-recruiting-what-companies-want) has a briefing template your coordinators can reuse.

## Throughout: track offers as they happen

Keep one sheet, updated the same day, with every student, every drive they sat, the rounds they cleared, and any offer. It answers the questions you will be asked every week: who is still unplaced, which students have more than one offer, and which departments need another round of outreach.

Agree on the offer policy before the first drive, in writing: how many offers a student can hold, when a student with an offer can sit for another company, and what happens when an offer is withdrawn. A policy decided mid-season always looks like favouritism to someone.

## After the season: write down what happened

The last task is the one most cells skip. Within a month of the season closing, write one page per department: baseline, what was done, drives held, offers made, and what you would change. Next year's plan starts from that page instead of from memory.

A semester plan will not make an unprepared batch ready in a week. What it does is move the work to the months where it still changes the outcome, and leave the season itself for what it should be: turning up, running the rounds, and recording the offers.
