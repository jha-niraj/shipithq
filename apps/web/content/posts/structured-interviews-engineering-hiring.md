An unstructured interview feels like the most human part of hiring: a good conversation, a gut feeling, a decision. It is also, in the research, one of the weakest ways to predict how someone will perform.

Structure fixes most of that, and it costs less than people fear.

## What "structured" means

A structured interview has four properties:

1. **The same questions** for every candidate for the role, in the same order.
2. **Questions tied to the job**, from a written list of what the role needs.
3. **A scoring rubric** for each question, written before any interview, with examples of strong, acceptable and weak answers.
4. **Independent scores**, recorded by each interviewer before they discuss the candidate.

That is it. It can still be warm, conversational and friendly. Structure constrains what you ask and how you judge, not how you behave.

## Why it works

In a 2022 re-analysis of decades of selection studies ([Sackett et al., Journal of Applied Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000994)), structured interviews had the highest estimated validity of the procedures examined, while unstructured interviews sat far lower. Google's people team reached a similar conclusion in practice and published its guidance on [structured interviewing](https://rework.withgoogle.com/guides/hiring-use-structured-interviewing/steps/introduction/).

The mechanism is simple: when every candidate answers the same question against the same rubric, the scores measure the candidates. When the questions differ, the scores measure the conversations.

## Writing the questions

Two kinds work well for engineers:

**Past behaviour.** "Tell me about a time you had to ship with a known bug. What did you decide and why?" Follow-ups are allowed but should also be fixed ("What would you do differently?").

**Situational.** "A service you own starts timing out at 3am and you are on call. Walk me through the first 20 minutes." The answer shows priorities and method, not trivia.

Avoid questions with a single correct answer that can be memorised, and avoid anything that tests something other than the job (hobbies, where someone went to school).

## Writing the rubric

For each question, write three anchors:

- **Strong:** "Names the trade-off explicitly, gives the reason, and describes how they checked the outcome."
- **Acceptable:** "Describes a reasonable decision but not the reasoning behind it."
- **Weak:** "Cannot give a concrete example, or blames others without describing their own part."

Score each answer on a small scale, 1 to 4 works well because it removes the easy middle.

## Running the debrief

Collect every interviewer's scores before anyone speaks. Then discuss the largest disagreements first, with the evidence each person wrote down. The loudest opinion in the room is not evidence.

## Voice rounds and consistency

A voice interview that asks every candidate the same questions, and scores them against the same rubric, is a structured interview at scale. It removes the interviewer who was tired, or in a hurry, or had a great chat about a shared hobby.
