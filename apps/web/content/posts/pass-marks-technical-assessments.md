A pass mark is the most consequential number in your hiring process and usually the least considered one. Someone picks 70% because it sounds reasonable, and from then on it silently decides who you never meet.

This guide covers how to set it on purpose, and how to check it is doing what you meant.

## What a pass mark is for

A pass mark answers one question: **below this score, is the evidence strong enough that this person cannot do the job?** It is not "the score of a good candidate". A good candidate should clear it comfortably. It marks the floor.

That distinction changes the number. If you set the pass mark where you picture a good hire, you reject everyone who is merely competent on a bad day.

## Three ways to set it

**1. Expert judgement, item by item.** The oldest method in standard setting (usually called the Angoff method): for each question, a few engineers who know the role estimate the probability that a *minimally competent* candidate answers it correctly. The pass mark is the sum of those probabilities. It is slow, but it anchors the number to the job instead of to a feeling.

**2. Benchmark against people who already do the job.** Give the assessment to a handful of engineers already performing well in the role. If most of them would fail it, the bar or the test is wrong. Your pass mark should sit comfortably below the score of people you would hire again.

**3. Start with the platform default and adjust with data.** If you have neither the time nor the benchmark group, start from a moderate default (ShipItHQ's platform pipelines use 60) and revisit it after the first cohort, using the checks below.

## Hard gates need lower marks than you think

If a round is a hard gate, everyone below the mark is gone and you never learn whether they would have been good. That asymmetry argues for setting hard-gate marks conservatively and letting the later, richer rounds do the fine sorting.

A useful pattern:

- **early, cheap rounds** (aptitude, a first coding problem): hard gate, moderate mark
- **later, expensive rounds** (design, conversation): advisory, discussed together

See [how to design a technical interview process](/blogs/technical-interview-process-design) for choosing which rounds gate.

## Check it after the first cohort

Once 30 or more candidates have taken a round, look at three things:

1. **The distribution.** If almost everyone passes, the round is not screening. If almost everyone fails, check the questions before you blame the candidates.
2. **Agreement with later rounds.** Candidates who barely passed the gate: how did they do later? If they do as well as those who passed easily, the gate is set too high.
3. **Adverse impact.** In the US, the EEOC's [Uniform Guidelines (29 CFR 1607.4)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XIV/part-1607/section-1607.4) describe the "four-fifths rule": a selection rate for any group below 80% of the highest group's rate is generally treated as evidence of adverse impact. Even outside the US it is a sensible alarm to watch.

## Time limits are part of the pass mark

A score out of time is not the same as a score in time. A 25-minute aptitude round with 20 questions and a pass mark of 60 is a different test from the same questions in 40 minutes. When you change one, you have changed the other.

Set the time so that a competent person finishes with a little room. You are testing reasoning, not typing speed.

## Retakes

Allow them, with a cool-down. People have bad days, and a retake after a day costs you nothing. It also discourages memorising: a candidate who retakes draws a fresh set from the pool.
