Every placement cell agrees that mock interviews help. Very few manage to give one to every student. The arithmetic is against them: a batch of a few hundred, a thirty-minute slot each, and faculty who are also teaching, setting papers and running labs. So mocks go to the students who ask first, who are rarely the ones who need them most.

This guide is a way to run mock interviews for a whole batch without burning out faculty: a fixed structure, a short rubric, a first pass that does not need a faculty member in the room, and faculty time spent where it matters most.

## Start with the structure, not the schedule

A mock is only useful if two students who sat it can compare notes and find they were tested on the same things. That means structure. Borrow it from how good companies interview: the same questions for every candidate, tied to the job, scored against a rubric written in advance. Google's [re:Work guides](https://rework.withgoogle.com/) have a readable case for structured interviewing, a 2022 re-analysis of selection research by Sackett and colleagues ([Journal of Applied Psychology, doi:10.1037/apl0000994](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000994)) placed structured interviews among the strongest predictors of job performance, and our guide to [structured interviews for engineering hiring](/blogs/structured-interviews-engineering-hiring) turns that into practice.

A mock for a final-year engineering student can fit in twenty-five minutes:

| Part | Time | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | 3 min | a clear, short account of who they are |
| Project walkthrough | 8 min | explaining a decision, handling a follow-up |
| Technical question | 10 min | reasoning out loud on one problem |
| Behavioural question | 4 min | a real example, told with structure |

Write the questions down. Keep a pool of several per part so answers do not circulate through the batch overnight, but make every question in the pool the same difficulty.

## A rubric small enough to use

A rubric with fifteen criteria gets filled in with the same number fifteen times. Use four, each scored 1 to 4:

1. **Clarity.** Could a stranger follow the explanation without interrupting?
2. **Depth on their own work.** Did they name a decision and a trade-off in their project?
3. **Reasoning.** On the technical question, did they think out loud, test their idea and recover from a wrong turn?
4. **Evidence in behavioural answers.** A specific situation, what they did, and what happened.

Write what a 1, 2, 3 and 4 look like for each, in observable terms. "Explains why they chose the database without being asked" is observable. "Good communication" is not. A 1 to 4 scale removes the comfortable middle score and forces a call.

## First pass: AI voice mocks for everyone

The first pass is where most of the volume is, and it is where faculty time is least needed. Most students in a first mock make the same few mistakes: answers that run for four minutes, a project described as a list of technologies, a behavioural story with no result. Nobody needs a professor to point those out.

This is the job AI voice mock interviews are suited for. A student sits a spoken mock with the same structured questions, on their own schedule, as many times as they want, and gets feedback against the same rubric. It gives every student in the batch a first rep, and a second and third, without booking a single faculty slot.

Be clear about the limits. An AI mock is good at structure, timing and the obvious gaps. It is not a substitute for an experienced engineer asking an unexpected follow-up about a project, and students should not treat its score as a prediction of placement. Use it as practice and as a filter, not as a verdict.

## Final rounds: faculty and alumni where they count

Once every student has had a first pass, route them:

- **Students scoring well on the first pass** get one faculty or alumni mock close to the season, to rehearse under real pressure.
- **Students stuck on one criterion** get a short targeted session: ten minutes on the project walkthrough is often enough.
- **Students who have not taken a first pass** get a nudge, not a faculty slot.

This inverts the usual pattern. Faculty stop spending their hours on basic advice that a first pass would have caught, and spend them on the conversations only an experienced interviewer can have. Alumni working in industry are the most underused resource here; a panel of five alumni giving two evenings each is a lot of final-round mocks.

## Close the feedback loop

A mock without feedback is a rehearsal of the same mistakes. Three rules:

- **Feedback within a day**, while the student still remembers what they said.
- **One thing to change**, not a list. A student who gets seven notes changes nothing.
- **A second mock**, on a fresh question, to check the change took. The movement between the first and second mock is the most useful number you will get.

At the batch level, pool the rubric scores by department. If one department scores low on project depth across the board, that is a coursework problem, not a mock problem. [Real projects in coursework](/blogs/project-based-learning-cs) is the fix for that one, and [measuring placement readiness](/blogs/placement-readiness-metrics) covers how to read these scores alongside the other signals.

## Protect the people running it

Faculty burn out on mocks for predictable reasons: too many slots, no rubric so every session is improvised, and no-shows. Cap each faculty member's slots per week, give them the rubric and question pool in advance, and require students to confirm the evening before. A faculty member who runs six well-prepared mocks a week for a month will do more good, and still be willing next year, than one who runs thirty in a week and never again.

The [Society for Human Resource Management](https://www.shrm.org/) publishes practical material on interviewer training that is worth sharing with volunteer interviewers. Finally, keep the mocks close to what companies actually ask. [What companies look for in campus hiring](/blogs/campus-recruiting-what-companies-want) is a good briefing for whoever writes the question pool.
