A junior engineering role can attract hundreds of applicants, and the usual response is a resume filter: a college list, a GPA cut-off, a keyword search. It is fast, and it quietly removes a large share of the people who would have done the job well.

This guide is about screening juniors on what they can do instead.

## Why resume filters fail for juniors

For experienced engineers, a resume at least describes past work. For juniors, it mostly describes circumstances: which college, which city, which internships were available to them. A keyword search then rewards the candidates who know which words to use, which is a skill, but not the one you are hiring for.

The result is a funnel that looks rigorous and selects for background.

## Replace the filter with a short, fair first round

Instead of reading 400 resumes, send every applicant the same short first round:

- **20 to 30 minutes**, no longer
- **the same for everyone**, randomised from a pool so answers do not circulate
- **automatically scored**, so volume costs you nothing
- **a clear pass mark**, set as a floor, not an ideal (see [setting pass marks](/blogs/pass-marks-technical-assessments))

An aptitude set or a single easy coding problem works well. It is cheap for candidates, cheap for you, and it measures the same thing for everyone.

## Then look at what they have built

For the candidates who clear the first round, projects are the best evidence a junior has. Ask for one they can talk about for ten minutes, and in the conversation ask about decisions, not features:

- Why this database and not another?
- What broke, and how did you find it?
- What would you change if you rebuilt it?

A candidate who built something small and can explain every decision is a stronger signal than one with a large, borrowed codebase they cannot explain.

## Keep the whole loop short

Juniors apply to many places at once and accept the first reasonable offer. A process that takes a month loses the best of them to the one that takes a week. Four rounds, one week, same loop for everyone: see [designing a technical interview process](/blogs/technical-interview-process-design).

## Check who you are filtering out

After the first cohort, compare pass rates across groups. The EEOC's [four-fifths guideline](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XIV/part-1607/section-1607.4) is a practical alarm: if one group passes at under 80% of the rate of the highest-passing group, look hard at the round before you blame the candidates.
