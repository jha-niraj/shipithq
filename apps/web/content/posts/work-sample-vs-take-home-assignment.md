"Give them a take-home" has become the default answer to "how do we see real work". Sometimes it is the right answer. Often it is a weekend of unpaid labour that tells you less than a well-built 45-minute work sample would.

This guide compares the two honestly, so you can pick per role.

## What each one is

**A work sample** is a short, timed task that looks like the job, done in a controlled setting: fix this failing function, extend this small API, review this pull request. It usually takes under an hour.

**A take-home assignment** is a larger task done on the candidate's own time: build a small app, a service, a feature. It usually takes several hours, often more than the brief admits.

## Why work samples are worth the effort

Work samples have long been among the strongest predictors of job performance in the selection research ([Schmidt and Hunter, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.124.2.262)). Even after the downward revisions in [Sackett et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000994), they remain useful, and they have something interviews lack: the candidate produces the actual thing.

They are also fair in a practical sense: every candidate gets the same task, the same time and the same scoring.

## Where take-homes go wrong

- **They filter for free time, not skill.** Candidates with caring responsibilities, second jobs or several processes running will drop out, and they are disproportionately the experienced ones.
- **The time is never what the brief says.** A "3-hour" task becomes eight, because the candidate knows polish is being judged.
- **You cannot tell who did the work.** Help from friends and from AI tools is invisible.
- **They are expensive to grade well.** Without a rubric, the reviewer rewards what they would have written.

## Where take-homes are the right tool

They earn their cost when the job genuinely involves sustained, independent work that a short task cannot show: structuring a project from nothing, writing documentation, making a sequence of decisions. For those roles:

1. **Cap the scope in writing** and say what you will not judge ("no styling needed").
2. **Pay for it** if it runs past two hours, or offer a live alternative.
3. **Score against a rubric written before any submission arrives.**
4. **Follow up with a conversation about it.** Ask why they made two or three specific decisions. It shows understanding, and it answers the "who did this" question better than any detector.

## A decision you can make in one line

- Screening at volume, or junior roles: **work sample**, timed, auto-checked where possible.
- Senior roles where independent structure is the job: **a short, capped take-home plus a conversation about it**.
- Never both as hard gates in the same loop. See [designing a technical interview process](/blogs/technical-interview-process-design).
