Students prepare for campus placements by folklore: a senior's advice, a list of questions shared on a group, a guess about which company asks what. The result is a batch that has over-prepared for some rounds and never heard of others. A placement cell can do better, because it sees the whole process from the recruiter's side of the drive.

This guide is for Training and Placement Officers, heads of department and placement coordinators. It covers the rounds campus hiring usually has, what companies are looking for in each, and how to brief students so they walk into each round knowing what it is for.

## Why campus hiring looks the way it does

A company visiting a campus faces a volume problem. It may see hundreds of applicants for a handful of roles, all fresh graduates with similar degrees and little work history. A resume says little about a fresher's ability, so most companies replace resume screening with a sequence of rounds that start cheap and automatic and end expensive and human.

Understanding that funnel is the most useful thing to tell students. Early rounds are about clearing a floor, quickly. Late rounds are about judgement. Preparing for them the same way wastes time on both. Our guide for companies on [hiring junior engineers without a resume filter](/blogs/hiring-junior-engineers-without-resume-filter) describes the same funnel from the employer's side, and is worth reading for anyone briefing students.

## The rounds, and what each one is for

### Online screening test

Usually aptitude plus some technical multiple choice or a short coding section, taken online, often on the college's lab machines. It exists to reduce hundreds of applicants to a number the company can interview. Companies generally set a floor rather than hunting for the top score.

**What to tell students:** speed and accuracy on standard aptitude topics matter more than cleverness. Do timed practice, not untimed reading. Do not leave questions blank if there is no negative marking, and check whether there is.

### Coding round

One or more problems with hidden test cases, anywhere from easy to medium difficulty for most service and product roles. Some companies watch the code as well as the result.

**What to tell students:** correct code on an easy problem beats a clever half-solution on a hard one. Handle edge cases, such as empty input, before submitting. Practise in the language they will actually use, on the kind of editor they will get. Timed practice on a fixed list of problem patterns beats solving problems at random.

### Technical interview

A conversation with an engineer: a problem or two on a whiteboard or shared editor, questions on core subjects such as operating systems, databases and networks, and almost always questions on a project.

**What to tell students:** think out loud. An interviewer cannot give credit for reasoning they cannot hear. Know one project deeply, including why they made the choices they made. [Real projects in coursework](/blogs/project-based-learning-cs) covers what those project questions look like.

### System design basics

Freshers are not expected to design large distributed systems. Some product companies do ask a light design question: how would you store this data, what happens when two users edit the same thing, how would you make this page load faster. The test is whether the student can reason about trade-offs, not recite architecture diagrams.

**What to tell students:** start with the requirements, draw the simplest thing that works, and then say where it would break. Naming one trade-off honestly is worth more than a diagram of ten boxes.

### Behavioural and HR round

Questions about teamwork, conflict, failure, and why this company. Many companies use structured questions and a rubric here, the same as in the technical rounds.

**What to tell students:** prepare four or five real stories from projects, internships, clubs or events, each with a situation, what they did and what happened. Research the company for ten minutes before the drive. "Why us?" answered with the company's own product in the answer stands out.

## What sits behind every round

Across the rounds, companies are looking for a short list of things that the National Association of Colleges and Employers describes in its research on [career readiness](https://www.naceweb.org/): critical thinking, communication, teamwork, professionalism and technology skills. The rounds are simply different ways of sampling those. A student who understands that is less likely to treat the HR round as a formality or the coding round as a memory test.

For a wider view of hiring practice, the [Society for Human Resource Management](https://www.shrm.org/) publishes extensively on selection and interviewing, and industry body [NASSCOM](https://nasscom.in/) publishes on skills demand in the Indian technology sector.

## How to brief students before a drive

A written brief, sent two or three days before each drive, removes most of the avoidable failures. Keep it to one page:

1. **The company in three lines.** What it does, which role, which location.
2. **The rounds, in order,** with the format and timing of each, as far as the company has shared them.
3. **Eligibility,** stated exactly, with the list of eligible students attached.
4. **Logistics.** Reporting time, room, what to bring, whether their own laptop is allowed.
5. **One preparation tip per round,** taken from the sections above.
6. **The offer policy,** so students know what accepting an offer means for later drives.

After the drive, spend ten minutes with the coordinator writing down what was actually asked. Over a season that becomes the most accurate preparation material the college owns, and it feeds straight back into [mock interviews for the batch](/blogs/mock-interviews-at-scale).

## Preparing the batch, not just the next drive

Briefs help students on the day. Readiness is built over months: timed practice, one finished project, a few mock interviews and a resume that survives a first read. The [semester plan for a placement season](/blogs/placement-season-plan) lays those out month by month, so that by the time companies arrive the batch already knows what each round is for.
